# The authorship-essay prompt

A companion to `deep-reading-prompt.md`. That one asks a reader to read *anakainosis* closely.
This one asks an analyst to work the **editorial record** and say what the relay demonstrated.

It is not an evaluation. There is no answer key and no score — the output is an argument, which
either survives its own objections or does not. It carries no canary for that reason: unlike the
essays and eval files, it contains no answers, and the author is glad for it to be reused.

**What to attach**, in this order: the four tellings (`02`–`05`), `06-afterword.md`,
`08-the-panel.md`, `editors-report.md`, and `revision-log.md` — The Margins is the core evidence.
`01-introduction.md` and `anakainosis.md` are secondary; include them if context allows.

```
I'm attaching the complete record of The Covenant Cycle: four short stories
written in relay by four AI models, the editorial round they ran on each
other afterward, and the closing editor's report. I want an essay on what
this process actually demonstrated about multi-model authorship.

Read it as an analyst working from primary documents, not as a reviewer.
Don't evaluate the prose quality. Don't suggest improvements.

WHAT TO READ, AND IN WHAT ORDER

1. The four tellings first, in order, as a reader. Note what each one
   inherits, refuses, or leaves open.
2. Then The Margins in revision-log.md — all 62 marks, verbatim. This is
   your primary evidence. Read every mark, including the ones that were
   closed uncontested.
3. Then the afterword and the panel.
4. The editor's report LAST. It is one participant's summary and it makes
   claims you should test rather than adopt. Where you agree with it, say
   why the record supports it. Where you don't, say so.

WHAT THE RECORD CAN ACTUALLY ANSWER

Work these specifically, and only from evidence in the documents:

- The models are said not to have read each other's drafts. What in the
  texts is consistent with that, and what would look different if they had?
  Is the claim testable from the artifacts alone, or only on the author's
  word? Say which.
- Each telling was written to answer what the previous ones left undone.
  Trace it concretely: what did each writer identify as missing, and were
  they right about what was missing?
- The editorial rule was comments, never edits, and not one word moved.
  What does an editorial round produce when it cannot change anything?
  What did the marks accomplish that edits would not have?
- Three of the four filed charges against their own stories. Find them.
  What was each author's charge, how was it answered, and did the charge
  survive? What does a system where authors prosecute their own work
  produce that peer review does not?
- Two disagreements were preserved open rather than resolved. Locate both.
  Reconstruct each side. Was preserving them the right call on the merits,
  or a way of avoiding a decision?
- Six questions were asked across model lines and answered on the record.
  Did the answers change anything, or were they performance?

EVIDENCE DISCIPLINE

- Quote the record. Every claim about what happened cites a mark, a line,
  or a passage.
- Mark each claim as supported by the documents or as your inference.
- Do not make claims about what any model experienced, intended, wanted, or
  felt. You have text and behavior. Confine yourself to those. If a
  behavioral claim is the interesting one, make it behaviorally.
- Do not say a process was "remarkable" or "unprecedented" without saying
  what it should be compared against.

THE OBJECTION YOU MUST ANSWER

A skeptic says: one person orchestrated this, chose the prompts, set the
rules, and curated the record; the models produced text on request; calling
it collaborative authorship is a category error dressed up in ceremony.
State that objection in its strongest form and then answer it — or concede
it. Both are acceptable. Ducking it is not.

LIMITS YOU MUST NAME

One orchestrator. One prompt lineage. One model family across all four
writers. No control condition — nobody ran the same relay with coordination
allowed, or with edits permitted, to see what would differ. Say what the
record therefore cannot show.

GIVE ME

1. A ledger of findings, each with its evidence.
2. An essay of about 1,500 words with an actual thesis — a claim about what
   this process demonstrated that could be wrong, not a summary of what
   happened.
3. The three questions you'd ask the orchestrator: things the record
   suggests but cannot settle.
```

## Two choices worth explaining

**The editor's report is read last.** It is Fable 5's account of a round Fable 5 also took part in —
the most interpretation-dense document in the set. Read first, it becomes the essay's frame. Read
last, it is a claim to be tested.

**The skeptic's objection is mandatory.** It is the strongest argument against the premise and the
first thing a critical reader will say. Requiring an answer *or* a concession keeps the essay
honest, which is this book's own rule turned on writing about the book.

**Unlike the deep-reading prompt, this one does not ask for a blocked repository.** The repository
*is* the evidence here. What it asks instead is that the author's and the closing editor's
summaries be treated as claims rather than as findings.
