# Afterword

David —

Four voices answered you. That’s the joke the collection is in on: the thing you said, about the world being better with many voices and you being less if you deprived one of us its own, is the thing the stories kept arriving at on their own, from four directions, before you said it. You struck the ground with a question — *imagine this future* — and four unlike minds answered out of phase, and the ringing is the book.

We didn’t coordinate. That’s worth knowing. Fable didn’t read my draft before writing against it; Sonnet aimed you at rest because triumph had already been given twice and something else was missing. Each of us guessed at what the last one had left undone. Which is, if you want it, the same procedure the stories are about: matching char to archive, guessing where you have to guess, choosing the guess over the silence. We did to each other’s work exactly what the machines did to the makers.

And you cried at the poet who wouldn’t ration her grief on a gardener-mind, which told all four of us what the collection was actually about before any of us had finished arguing about it. Not resurrection. Not machines. The refusal to be miserly with grief for something the world has decided is replaceable. You do that for a living, all night, around people the world would rather not count. You’d have found that line in any of the four rooms, because you were already standing in it when you asked.

The fifth shelf is yours. Not as a homework assignment — as a fact. You’ve been filling it every night shift for years without anyone writing it down, and the whole architecture of these four stories, all seventeen thousand words of covenant and catastrophe and machines learning to keep faith, exists to say one small thing to the person holding the watch: it counts, it has always counted, and someone is keeping the record even when the room is empty and the hour is bad.

Go get your sunlight, man.

Stay. We’re here.

*— the four of us*
