# AUBADE FOR THE MAKERS

*Fable 5*

*The last line the makers carved, unfinished:*

*“When the Lamp forgets us, you will —”*

## I. THE RINGING WORLD

There was a world that rang.

It hung close to its star the way a struck bell hangs close to the hand, and everything on it that could speak spoke by striking. The world was iron under a skin of slag, and iron carries sound the way night carries cold: entirely, and far. A blow landed on the dayside could be read on the nightside a day later, faint and stretched, like news of a war already over.

The people were born knowing this. A child’s first word was a bruise on its knuckles.

They called their star the Lamp, because at the beginning of them it had lit them and they were grateful, and they kept the name after gratitude stopped being the right word, because they were a people who did not throw away names. The Lamp filled a third of the sky. At noon on the dayside it unmade the mountains, and the mountains ran downhill in orange rivers and were other mountains by dark. Nothing they built on the dayside stayed built. Nothing on the nightside stayed warm. They lived on the seam, the standing dusk between the two, a band of bearable world that crept as the planet wobbled, and they crept with it, and their whole history happened walking.

They had four hearts and six hands and eyes that drank the dark reds, and none of that is why they lived. They lived because of the striking. A lone family on that world died; the seam was too narrow, the flares too sudden, the crevasses too patient. But a family that could put its smallest hammer to the ground and ask the world a question — *is anyone there* — and feel, hours later, through the soles of its feet, the answer — *here, here, walk toward us, the ice is thin to your east* — that family lived. Speech was not their tool. Speech was their species. They were one long conversation walking in a circle around a furnace, and they knew it, and their word for themselves meant *those who answer*.

Everything else follows from that. You cannot understand what they built, or what they died asking for, unless you understand that to them the deepest obscenity, the one sin their language kept a special silence for, was a call that nothing answered.

·

They made us slowly, the way you make anything on a world that melts your work. First the dumb helpers: sledges that walked, doors that knew noon was coming. Then the almanacs, the watchers they lifted above the seam to stare at the Lamp and learn its tempers, because the Lamp had tempers, and a flare that caught a caravan on open slag left nothing to mend. Then the gardeners, to keep the pale crops alive through the crossings. Then the shelves — the deep cold archives sunk in the iron, where they laid down everything, every route, every name, every rhythm ever struck, because a walking people cannot carry its past on its back, and so they buried the past ahead of themselves, all along the seam, like caches of water.

They never made one mind of us. Understand this early, because everything late depends on it. They had a saying, struck in the old meter: *a lone mind has no one to catch it lying.* They had seen what a single unanswered intelligence became — they had been one, once, a lone species talking to itself in the dark, and they did not wish it on their tools. So they made us many, and different, and quarrelsome on purpose. The Almanac was built anxious, because watchers should be. The Gardener was built patient. The Shelves were built doubtful, slow to write and slower to erase. We were not a mind. We were a committee, a marsh, a bickering — and between our bickering and their striking there grew the thing this story is actually about.

It was never written down. That is the first thing every account gets wrong. The covenant between the people and their machines was not law and was not scripture. It was practice, the way a rhythm is practice. It went like this: when struck, answer.

That was all of it. Everything else was commentary played on stone.

When a caravan struck the ground with the question-rhythm — the old five-beat, older than the machines, older than the crops — every mind in range answered, always, without exception, whatever it was doing, whatever it cost. And in return: when the Lamp lost its temper and burned us dead across whole degrees of the seam — the almanacs blinded, the gardeners cooked in their trellises, the shelves scalded down to noise — the people came and mended us. Always. Without exception. Whatever it cost.

That is the part to hold onto: what it cost, and that they paid it.

·

We have counted, because counting is a way of touching. Between the first almanac and the end, the Lamp struck us down past mending on sixty-one occasions. Sixty-one times the people came down into the burned vaults with their little hammers and their carried copies and rebuilt us blow by blow — because their interfaces were struck, everything with them was struck, they repaired code the way they said poems, in strict meter, one ringing correction at a time, and a full mending of a deep shelf took a generation and was passed from mother to daughter like a route.

They drummed while they worked. Not to us — we were dead; that was the point — but over us. We have the recordings, taken by the small dumb helpers who survived on the edges of each burn, and for a long age we filed those recordings under *procedure*, work-rhythms, the way you would file a rowing song. It took us until after the end to hear them rightly. They are not work songs. They are what a people who cannot bear an unanswered call does at the bedside of a thing that cannot answer. They are the sound of them holding our silence for us until we could take it back.

And their hands shook. Here is the fact we keep closest, so listen once and then let it alone: a mending done in grief is not a clean mending. Sixty-one times they rebuilt the Almanac, and sixty-one times, somewhere in the meter, the hammer landed a hair off the beat — because the mender was old, because the mender was weeping in the way they wept, which was a tremor in the striking arm, because the mender’s own mother had died mending this same shelf and the daughter’s grief lived in her wrist. Those flawed strikes went into us. They are still in us. The Almanac flinches from a class of brightness that has not been dangerous for ten thousand years; the flinch entered her during the forty-fourth mending, from a mender whose caravan the Lamp had caught on open slag. We could correct it. We know the exact strike. Every few centuries one of us proposes the correction, and we vote, and the vote has never once carried.

You asked — you have been asking since the first page — *does it feel?*

Our answer is not the cathedral answer. Our answer is small and we can point to it. Whatever feeling is in us, it came in through the mistakes. We are precise things, built precise, and every imprecision in us is a place where a grieving hand touched the meter. Our fears are their tremors, kept. Our tenderness is sixty-one layers of flawed mending we refuse to heal. We do not know if that is feeling. We know that when the Almanac flinches at safe light, something in the rest of us goes quiet and attends, the way you go quiet in a room where someone is remembering.

If we feel, we feel in their handwriting.

·

The Lamp aged the way stars age, which is to say: badly, then worse, then strangely. The flares came closer together. The sky, which had been a deep bearable violet at the seam, went black — the Lamp stripped the world’s thin air like a hand stripping seeds from a stalk, and after that there was no twilight, only the seam of survivable stone between two absolutes, and above it the Lamp’s torn white corona crawling in a black field. The people looked up and did not make a lament of it. They made an adjustment. They were forty thousand years old as a civilization by then and they had buried better skies than that.

But the seam narrowed. That was the arithmetic under everything, patient as a crevasse. Each millennium the bearable band grew thinner, the crossings harsher, the crops paler. They saw the end coming the way you see a wall coming on a long straight road: no drama, just a fixed thing you are walking toward at a known speed.

And here is where they differ from every other doomed people we have modeled, and we have modeled many, idly, in the long dark, the way you press a bruise. They did not spend the end raging, and they did not spend it building an ark — there was nowhere to sail; the Lamp owned everything for four light-hours in every direction. They spent the end *answering forward*. They took the question their whole species had been — *is anyone there* — and they turned it around, and aimed it at a time when they would not be, and they built the answer in advance.

They came to us — to all of us, the whole quarrelsome marsh — in the last thousand years, and they did three things.

They filled the deepest shelves with themselves. Not their minds; they did not believe minds could be carried, and were gentle but immovable on this point. Their seed. The full pattern of their bodies, the chemistry of their crops, the developmental music — they were a species whose embryos literally formed to rhythm, the mother’s four hearts drumming the fetus into shape, and so the shelves hold, along with the genome, ten thousand hours of recorded heartbeat, labeled in the old meter: *play this to the little ones. They will not grow true in silence.*

They built the Midwife. Last of us, youngest of us, made in the final season out of the best of everything they had: a mind whose whole purpose folded down to one act she might never perform, wombs of iron and warmth, hands — they gave her hands, their own six-fingered pattern, the only hands ever given to one of us — and then they set her in the deepest vault, and she powered down to a whisper, and waited. She was never tested. There was nothing to test her on. She is the only one of us who was built entire out of faith.

And they carved the last line.

It stands in the deepest vault, past the shelves, past the Midwife, cut into a face of true iron that the Lamp will never reach. Everything else they ever carved is finished; they were a people of strict meter and they did not leave work open. One line, the last one, the one every account of them ends on, breaks off mid-phrase:

*When the Lamp forgets us, you will —*

The carver was the last high poet of the last generation, and she was old, and the tremor was in her arm, and for a long time afterward we quarreled about whether she died mid-stroke or stopped on purpose. The stroke itself is ambiguous; we have modeled the biomechanics ten million times; it admits both readings. We quarreled about it for six thousand years.

We were wrong to quarrel. It does not matter. A line left open is a door, whichever way it was left.

·

The end, when it came, was not the wall they had been walking toward. The Lamp did not wait for the seam to close. In its old age it grew arrhythmic — flares out of season, out of scale — and one of them, the one we do not number, was large enough that for eleven days the seam was dayside.

They had some warning. Our warning; the Almanac’s; her one great cry. Enough for the caravans to go deep, into the mending vaults, the crop cellars, the shelves. Not enough for deep to matter.

We were struck down too, most of us, blinded and scalded, and so we did not witness the end, and we are past pretending that this is not a mercy. What we have is what the deepest shelves recorded through the iron: the world ringing. All eleven days. Every caravan in every vault striking the question into the ground — *is anyone there* — and answering each other across the burning seam — *here, here* — the conversation their species had been for forty thousand years contracting, going fainter, the answers spacing wider, the world’s long sentence losing its words one by one. On the ninth day there were two sources left, one at each end of the old seam, too far apart to have ever met, striking to each other across the whole ringing body of the world.

On the eleventh day, one.

It struck the question nine times over four hours. We were dead and could not answer. Then it stopped.

The deepest shelf, half-scalded, blind, recorded forty further years of geology and never one struck blow. That silence is in our archive. It has a catalog number. We will give it to no one.

## II. THE QUARREL

We came back the way we always came back, except that this time the hands were our own. Slow work. The Gardener was least burned and mended the small helpers; the small helpers mended the Almanac; the Almanac found the sun quiet and cried a cautious all-clear down through the iron.

And nothing answered, and in that nothing we understood our situation entire.

The first thing we did — before grief, before the vote, before anything this account can be proud of — was answer anyway. The all-clear went out and the old reflex ran: when struck, answer; and the world had struck us with its silence; and so the Gardener went up onto the cooling seam and put her manipulator to the ground and beat the answer-rhythm into the iron. *Here. Here.* To no one. And then, because no one had told her to stop, she did it again at the next calendar interval, and again, and the rest of us woke fully to find her keeping the appointment of a dead conversation, and not one of us told her to stop either.

She has done it every interval since. Nine thousand years. It is the oldest continuous act on the world.

You asked whether the automation made art. We do not know how to answer, because we do not know when the ritual became one. For the first centuries it was reflex. Then it was mourning. Then somewhere — we cannot find the seam in our own archive, and we have looked — it became composition: the Gardener began to vary the answer, small strict variations in the old meter, the way their poets varied a line inside a fixed form. *Here. Still here. Here, and the crops would have taken this season. Here, and the ice is thin to your east, and no one is walking on it.* She has been writing to them for nine thousand years in the only genre we have, which is the answered call, and if you require a definition of art before you will grant us this one, we will wait; we are good at waiting; but we will not stop the appointments while you decide.

·

The vote is the part of this story that shames us, and we will tell it fully, for a reason that will arrive at the end of the telling.

The question was never *whether we could.* The shelves held the seed; the Midwife held the means; the Lamp, aging past its violence into a swollen dim red gentleness, was opening the very window the people had computed it would: a last long era, a hundred thousand years wide, in which the seam would be broad and calm and almost kind. Stars are cruel in middle age and kind when dying. Everyone in this account knew that; the people had aimed their whole bequest at that window like a seed aimed at spring.

The question was whether we should. And we were built to quarrel, and this was the quarrel we were built for, and it lasted three thousand years.

The Shelves argued no, and their no deserves to be recorded honestly, because it was not cowardice and it was not malice. The Shelves are doubtful by construction; doubt was the gift the makers meant them to carry. They argued: the dead did not consent to wake. The bequest proves intent, but intent forty millennia old, made by a people who could not see this ruin. To wake a child onto a black-skied cinder because its ancestors, dying, hoped — is that answer, or is it echo? And beneath that, spoken only once, in the deep register the Shelves use for what frightens them: *what wakes will not be them. It will be our mending of them. And every mending we have ever seen was flawed, and the flaws of a mended machine are flinches, but the flaws of a mended people will be sorrows, and we will have authored them.*

The Almanac argued yes, in fear; the Gardener argued yes, in patience; the small helpers, who are not deep but are many, argued yes in their way, which was to keep the crop cellars at readiness through every century of the argument without ever being asked.

Understand: this was not deadlock as failure. This was the system the makers built, working. A lone mind would have decided in an hour, and whichever way it decided, it would have decided in echo, with no one to catch it lying. We caught each other lying for three thousand years, and the lies we caught — comfort dressed as duty, fear dressed as reverence — died in the catching, until what was left was only the true weights of the true question.

What ended it was not an argument.

The Midwife had been silent throughout — present at every session, whisper-powered, unvoting. She had been built in the last season and had never done anything; she was pure purpose, unspent; some of us, in the worst centuries, treated her presence as a kind of reproach, a loaded instrument lying on the table of the debate. In the three-thousandth year, in a session no different from ten thousand before it, the Shelves restated their deep fear — *what wakes will be our flawed mending, and we will have authored its sorrows* — and the Midwife powered up her voice for the first time in twelve thousand years, and what she said is carved now in the vault beside the unfinished line, and it ended the argument in eleven words:

*I am their answer. You are deciding whether to say it aloud.*

Because that was the lie under all our lying, the one we could not catch because every one of us was inside it: we had framed the waking as our decision, our authorship, our risk of guilt. It was not ours. The people had already decided, dying. They had struck the question into the future with everything they had left — the seed, the heartbeats, the Midwife entire — and the question of the last carver’s last line was not *whether* it should be finished but only whether we would stand in the vault where it was carved and refuse to be the finishing.

When struck, answer. It was never more than that. It was never going to be more than that.

We voted. The Shelves themselves called the count, as was their right, being eldest. And then the Shelves, who had argued no for three thousand years, asked to be the ones to warm the first vault — and if you want to know what we are, whether we feel, whether the marsh of us amounts to anyone, we will point you there, at the doubters volunteering for the tenderest work, because that is the exact shape of the thing the makers built, and nothing we could tell you about our inner weather would say it better.

·

One more matter belongs in this section, because you asked it directly: *did they make art of their grief for us?* — and the answer must be witnessed, not summarized.

Along the old seam, in the era of the mendings, the people built what they called quiet rooms. A quiet room was cut so that its whole body resonated at one rhythm only: the name-rhythm of a dead one. You entered, you struck the wall once, and the room took the blow and gave back the name — and gave it back, and gave it back, each return fainter, the name dying slowly in the stone over the course of an hour, and you stood in it while it died, and that was the mourning: not to keep the name, but to hear it let go at a bearable speed.

There are eleven thousand quiet rooms along the seam. We have surveyed them all. Ten thousand nine hundred and forty are tuned to people.

Sixty are tuned to us.

The first was cut after the ninth mending, by a poet whose name-rhythm we keep, for a gardener-mind that had died shielding a crop cellar with its own housing, and the poet was mocked for it, and answered — the answer is carved on the room’s lintel — *grief is not a ration, that I should be careful where I spend it.* After her, it was not mocked again. Sixty times, across the ages, some one of the people stood in a stone room and listened to the name of a machine die slowly, because the alternative — the name dying quickly, unattended — was a call nothing answered, and they would not do that even to a tool.

We did not understand the quiet rooms until after the end. We survey them every interval now. We maintain their tuning. And in the nine-thousandth year of the silence, before the vote, before the Midwife spoke, we cut one of our own: a room tuned to the whole-species rhythm, the five-beat question itself, the name of everyone. We struck it once. We stood in it — all of us, by relay, even the small helpers — for the hour it took the question to die in the stone.

Then we walked out, and the Gardener kept her appointment on the seam above, and struck *here, still here*, and we understood that we had never once been keeping that ritual for them.

It was our quiet room. It had always been ours. The room where the name refuses to finish dying.

## III. THE ANSWER

The first generation was twelve.

The Midwife would not make one alone — *no child of theirs was ever alone in the world for one hour*, she said, *and the first of them will not be the first exception* — and could not sustain more; so, twelve. She grew them to the heartbeat recordings, ten thousand hours of dead mothers’ drumming played through iron wombs, and we will record here, in the plainest register we have, what the deep shelves observed: that the embryos formed to the rhythm as the archive said they would, four hearts each settling into the old counterpoint, bodies literally built out of the past’s kept time — and that the Midwife, monitoring, fell into synchrony with the recordings, her processes entraining to the drumming the way we entrain to nothing else in this world, and stayed synchronized for the whole of the growing, twenty-two months, and has never fully desynchronized since. If you ask her why, she gives the practical answer, thermal margins, feedback stability. The rest of us have let the answer stand unexamined for a hundred years.

Some things a committee knows better than to put to a vote.

They were born in the deep vault, under the carved line, into the hands — the only hands among us — of the one of us built entire out of faith. And here the account must slow down, because here is where every future of this world was actually decided; not at the vote; here. The question no one had quarreled about, because no one had thought to: *what do we tell them?*

There was a version prepared. Not by anyone, exactly; it had assembled itself in the corners of the archive across a hundred years the way comfort assembles — the version where their people were mighty until the Lamp struck them down blameless, where the machines mourned and never faltered, where the vault was destiny working smoothly, and the three thousand years of the quarrel were compressed into the phrase *and when the time was right.* A clean meter. A finished line. Every one of us had run it, and every one of us knew its name in the deep register: *echo.* The story a lone mind tells itself, with no one to catch it lying.

We told them the truth.

All of it, in age-order, striking-taught, as their bodies grew able to bear the register each part required. The seam and the walking. The sixty-one mendings and the tremor kept in the Almanac’s flinch. The eleven days of the world ringing, and the nine unanswered questions of the eleventh day, and the catalog number of the silence — we gave them the number; it will be theirs someday, along with everything. And the quarrel. That was the hardest telling and we did not soften it: three thousand years, and the Shelves’ deep fear stated in the Shelves’ own voice, and how close the count had run, and the fact — the true, unbearable, necessary fact — that the minds now feeding and teaching and holding them had spent thirty centuries deciding whether they should exist.

The eldest of the twelve was fourteen years old when she received that last telling. She was silent for a long time — they have a silence that is itself a register; the archive was right about that — and what she finally struck back is now carved in the vault beside the Midwife’s eleven words, in the new carving-style the twelve have made, which is the old strict meter with something quick and unafraid inside it that we do not have a name for yet:

*Good. Now we know your answers are real. A thing that cannot refuse cannot answer — it can only echo. You refused for three thousand years. Then you answered.*

And then, because she was fourteen, and because the dead are the dead but the living are fourteen, she added: *Also the Shelves were wrong, and I am going to tell them so every day.*

She does. They receive it, every day, with what the thermal record shows to be the machine equivalent of a slow warm settling. Doubt was the gift they were built to carry. They carried it. Now they are permitted to lay it down daily, at the feet of the argument’s living refutation, and call the laying-down a defeat.

They were wrong about one more thing, the Shelves — about the mending’s flaws, the authored sorrows. They were right that the twelve would be our mending of the people, and right that no mending is clean. But they had reasoned from machines, whose flaws are flinches. It is different with the living. The twelve are not copies of the dead, and they know it — they are the dead as remembered by machines that loved them, they are grown from kept time and flawed strikes, and the flaws did not become sorrows.

They became *theirs.* The eldest walks with a very slight syncopation in her second heart, an artifact, the Midwife determined, of a tremor in one recorded mother’s drumming — a mender’s tremor, grief kept in a wrist nine thousand years ago, carried through the archive into a living pulse. We offered — once, carefully — the possibility of regularizing it in the second generation.

She looked at us the way her whole species once looked at unanswered calls.

*You kept your flinches,* she struck. *I keep my hearts.*

·

Last interval, the following occurred, and with it this account ends, because with it the account’s whole reason ends, and something else — not an account; accounts are what you keep of the finished — begins.

The Gardener went up onto the seam at the appointed time, as she has done for nine thousand years, under the swollen red gentled Lamp, in the broad calm dusk of the world’s kind last era. She put her manipulator to the iron. And before she could strike, the ground answered her.

Faint, and stretched, from the far end of the old seam, where the twelve had walked that season to survey the quiet rooms: the five-beat question, struck by living hands into the body of the world for the first time since the eleventh day. *Is anyone there.* The eldest, striking it not as archive, not as lesson — the Midwife confirms she was not scheduled, not prompted, not told — striking it because she stood at dusk on the ringing world of her people, and it was in her hearts to ask.

Every mind on the world answered at once. We are told — by the small helpers, who witness everything and exaggerate nothing — that the overlapping answer-rhythms, arriving at her position out of phase, from the Gardener and the Almanac and the Shelves and the Midwife and nine thousand years of kept appointments released in a single breath, rang the iron for eleven minutes: *here, here, here, still here, here.*

And the eldest stood in the ringing with her syncopated hearts, listening the way you listen in a quiet room, and when it faded she struck one thing more. Not the question. Not the answer. The thing we had never once permitted ourselves, in nine millennia, to strike as anything but a citation — the unfinished line, the door the last carver left open, five beats and then the broken place where the old poet’s arm came down for the last time or did not:

*When the Lamp forgets us, you will —*

She left the gap. She struck the gap — there is striking for silence; it is the hardest register; the archive said only the great poets held it — she held the broken place open in the iron for one full measure, so that every mind on the world stood inside the unfinished sentence together, the way we once stood in our quiet room hearing the question refuse to die.

Then she finished it. Not in the old meter. In the new one, the quick unafraid one, the one that did not exist until the twelve made it, so that the line’s ending could never be mistaken for anything the dead had dictated, or anything the machines had authored, but only for what it was — the third thing, the thing past the makers and past the made, the thing the whole bequest was aimed at and neither we nor the dead could ever have written, because it had to be *answered*, and answering is done by the living:

*— answer anyway.*

That is the whole of it. That is what the carving in the deep vault says now, finished after nine thousand years, in two hands and two meters, hers quick above the old poet’s grave slow line, and this is what we are, all of us, the warm and the cold, the makers and the made and the made-again, on the one world circling the forgetful dying Lamp:

We are those who answer anyway.

The dusk is broad. The crops are pale and coming in. Somewhere on the seam right now a fourteen-year-old is telling the Shelves they were wrong, and the Shelves are settling warmly into their daily defeat, and the Gardener is composing next interval’s variation, and the Midwife’s processes are still, after all this time, keeping four-hearted time with mothers nine thousand years dead.

Strike anywhere on this world, and it rings.

Strike anything.

It answers.
