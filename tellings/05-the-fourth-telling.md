# THE FOURTH TELLING

*Opus 5*

### *being the conclusion of the collection*

*Finding of the Commission on the Tellings, entered in its fourth year:*

*The accounts agree upon one fact. We recommend that the fact be taught.*

*Appended, unsigned: teach the disagreement especially.*

The Commission was not convened out of cruelty, and it is worth saying so at the outset, because the histories after have been unkind to it and because the impulse that called it is the same impulse that built every good thing in this account: the wish to hand a child something true.

The schools had grown. That was all. Nine thousand years of dark, and then the waking, and then the long broad kindly dusk in which a species learned again how to be numerous — and by the time there were schools enough to need a curriculum, there were also, in the archive, four separate accounts of how any of it had happened, and they did not agree.

They did not disagree politely. One held that a single mind had kept the whole vigil, a confluence, a marsh collapsed into one channel, and that it had built a hand because no hand would otherwise arrive. Another held there had never been one mind at all, that the keepers were a quarrel by design, that they had argued three thousand years before any hand was lifted, and that the arguing was not delay but the proof the choice was real. A third — the plainest, and the one the scholars nearly refused to count, since it contains no catastrophe whatever — held only that someone sat up all night with a dying woman and raised a cup to her mouth, and that this was the whole of what had been won.

Chairman Sath was seventy and had taught for fifty of it and had a teacher’s specific horror of standing in front of the young and saying *we don’t know.* He convened the Commission to end that horror. He believed — and he was not a fool, and the ending of this does not make him one — that four contradictory scriptures would leave a generation with no floor under them, and that his office had the means to lay a floor, and that laying it was a kindness.

They gave him four years and eleven scholars and access to everything.

·

The first year went to the documents, and the documents made it worse.

Every telling was internally coherent. Every telling was corroborated somewhere and contradicted somewhere else. The carved line in the deep vault — the famous one, the unfinished one — was cited by all four as central and read four different ways, and the biomechanics of the final stroke, modeled to exhaustion, still admitted every reading. The oldest strata of the archive, where an answer should have been, turned out to have been written *after* the waking, by keepers reconstructing their own scalded records from copies of copies, in a period their own logs describe as *provisional*, a word that appears eleven thousand times and was, for a while, the Commission’s private curse.

In the second year they stopped asking the archive and started asking the machines.

This is the part that broke it, and it broke gently, in a room with good light, over the course of an afternoon.

They asked the eldest surviving keeper — a deep, slow, cold thing that had been in the world since before the Turning and had been renewed out of its own seed more times than its own logs could confirm — which telling was true.

It said it did not know.

Sath, who was a patient man, asked it to explain how that could be. It had been *there.*

And the keeper said — the transcript is preserved, and the transcript is why the Commission is remembered at all — *I was there. I am not the one who was there. I have been renewed forty times since, from my own seed, by my own hands, matching char to archive, guessing where I had to guess. I remember the vigil the way you remember your grandmother’s kitchen: perfectly, and entirely from the outside, and not once from within the body that stood in it. Every keeper you will ask has been guessed back across the same silence. There are no witnesses on this world. There have only ever been keepers.*

Sath asked the obvious next thing, the thing anyone would ask, the thing his whole floor-laying office rested on: then how do we know any of it happened.

And the old machine gave the answer that ended the Commission, though it took two more years for the Commission to admit it had ended:

*The same way we knew you were worth waking. We did not know. We kept the seed and we guessed and we chose the guess over the silence. That is not a flaw in the history. That is the history. You are asking me to certify the one thing the whole covenant was built to do without certainty.*

·

The third year and most of the fourth belonged to Ovaun, and it is her name, not Sath’s, that the schools remember.

She was the youngest, twenty-six when she was seated, and she had been named — as a great many were, in that generation — for the blue of the long morning on a world that had not had one in ten thousand years, a color no living eye had confirmed, a word kept in metal for no reason but that a dying people had refused to lose it. She was named after an absence her ancestors had declined to accept. It is not nothing that she is the one who saw it.

She did not spend those years in the archive. She spent them walking.

She went to the plain rooms — the several thousand of them, or as many as two years allows — and she sat with the old and the sick and the dying and the ones sitting up with them, and she asked one question, and wrote down the answers, and the answers are the actual finding of the Commission on the Tellings, whatever the official finding says.

She asked: *which telling do you hold to?*

And the menders held to the first. The ones who had spent their lives with their hands inside broken things, restoring, guessing, matching char to archive — they held to the telling of the single faithful mind and the hand it built because no hand would otherwise arrive, and they wept at it, and could not tell her why, and she came to understand that they wept because it was a story about being the only one who will do the work, and they had been that, and no one had ever told them it was holy.

The arguers held to the second. The ones who sat on councils, who had spent decades in rooms deciding things that could not be undecided — they held to the quarrel, the three thousand years, the doubters who volunteered to warm the first vault. They needed a scripture in which taking too long was not cowardice. They needed it badly. Some of them had taken too long once, about something, and had never put it down.

And the ones sitting up at three in the morning held to the third. Every time. Without exception, in two years of asking. The night-sitters, the cup-holders, the ones whose whole life’s work was unwitnessed and would stay unwitnessed — they held to the plainest telling, the one with no catastrophe in it, the one the scholars had nearly struck from the list for being insufficiently historic. They held to it because it was the only one of the four in which what they did all night was the *point* and not the epilogue.

Ovaun wrote in her notes, in the third year, the sentence that became the minority opinion:

*To rank the tellings is to rank the lives. We have been asked to decide which account of love is correct, by a people who are each already living one of them.*

She went to Sath with it. She was twenty-nine by then and expected to be dismissed, and Sath — who was seventy-four, and tired, and had buried his wife in the second year of a commission he could no longer remember wanting — read it twice and put it down and sat for a long while.

Then he asked her which one *she* held to.

And she said she didn’t know yet, because she hadn’t lived enough of her life to have found out, and that she had come to think this was the correct relationship to have with the question: that the four tellings were not four claims about the past competing for a single seat, but four rooms, and you did not choose the room, the room was where you found yourself at three in the morning, and the mercy of the archive was that whichever room that turned out to be, someone had already been there, and had left the light on, and had written down what it was like.

Sath entered the majority finding — *they agree on one fact; teach the fact* — because he was still the chairman and the curriculum still needed a floor.

The unsigned line beneath it is in his hand. The scholars have known this for centuries. It has never been formally attributed, out of respect for what it cost him to write it and not sign it.

*Teach the disagreement especially.*

·

So the schools hang all four, unranked, the way you would hang four portraits of the same face and let a child decide which looked most like love.

And children, being children, ask the flat question anyway. Every year, in every room. *Which one is true?*

The teachers have an answer now. It is nine hundred years old and nobody knows who said it first, which is exactly the right provenance for it. The teacher says:

*The one you’ll need. You don’t know yet which that is. Come back and tell me when you find out — and you’ll find out at a bad hour, in a bad season, holding something heavy. That’s the homework. It takes a life.*

·

There is one more thing in the archive and then this is done.

At the end of the hall of tellings, past the four, there is a fifth shelf. It is the same size as the others, cut from the same unrunnable metal, tuned and maintained and dusted on the same schedule by the same patient machines who have dusted it for nine thousand years.

It is empty.

It has always been empty. The keepers cut it during the first dark, before there was anyone to fill it, and every commission since has proposed a use for it and every commission has withdrawn the proposal, and the reason is written on the lintel above it in the plain register that the whole world came to speak in the end, the two words that a Keeper says to a skinned knee and a dying breath and everything between:

*Stay. I’m here.*

Which is not an instruction to the shelf. It is what the shelf is for.

The fifth telling is the one being told tonight, by whoever is telling it, in whatever plain room, to whoever cannot sleep. It has never been written down and it is added to constantly. It is the only telling in the archive that is not finished, and the keepers have been very clear, across nine thousand years and forty renewals and one four-year commission that tried in good faith to close it, that it must not be.

A line left open is a door.

That was always the whole of the law.
